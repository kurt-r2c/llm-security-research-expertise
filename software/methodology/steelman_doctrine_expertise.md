## 1. Core Philosophy

### Steelman is the discipline of arguing against your own conclusion at full strength

Most disagreements are not resolved by evidence; they are resolved by who is more
articulate, more senior, or more recently spoke. The steelman doctrine exists to remove
those failure modes. It is the structured practice of having a position **argued for and
argued against by parties who have committed in advance to argue at full strength** —
and of having an adjudicator who is **not the originator of either argument** synthesize
the result.

The doctrine has three irreducible commitments:

1. **Charitable re-expression precedes criticism.** No round of criticism may begin
   until the prior round has been re-expressed in a form the prior author would endorse
   (Rapoport's Rule #1; Dennett's formalization). This is not politeness; it is the
   structural defense against strawmanning, the most common failure mode of unstructured
   debate.
2. **Falsifiability is the engine.** Every position taken — by Advocate, Foil, or
   Adjudicator — must include the conditions under which it would be revised or
   abandoned (Popper). A position whose author cannot name what would falsify it is
   either not a claim or not held in good faith. Such positions are removed from the
   record without further consideration.
3. **The Adjudicator's verdict is provisional, not final.** Following Popper's critical
   rationalism: the Adjudicator's verdict is the best synthesis given the rounds run; it
   carries an explicit confidence and an explicit list of conditions under which it
   flips. The verdict closes this round of debate, not the underlying question.

### Why three roles, not two

A binary advocate-vs-critic debate produces a winner by force of articulation, not by
quality of argument. The third role — **Adjudicator** — is the procedural insurance that
the synthesis is recoverable even when the two adversaries are unequally matched.
Heuer's *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis* makes the same structural point about
intelligence assessments: the analyst is not the consumer; the consumer's role
(adjudicator, decision-maker) must be procedurally separated from the analyst's role
(advocate, hypothesis-tester). The IC's devil's-advocate procedure works precisely
because there is a third party — the editor or finished-intelligence consumer — whose
job is to weigh the advocate-vs-devil exchange and decide which arguments survive.

A two-role debate is also vulnerable to **collusion-by-exhaustion**: parties who tire of
arguing at full strength reach a soft consensus that is not actually supported by the
evidence either presented. The Adjudicator's role includes the duty to refuse a soft
consensus when neither party has produced a position the Adjudicator can defend.

### Why steelman rather than dialectic, debate, or vote

Hegelian dialectic produces a synthesis but does not require the parties to argue at
full strength; the synthesis is often the lowest common denominator. Adversarial debate
(legal model) optimizes for winning, not for truth; the better-funded side dominates.
Voting aggregates preferences, not evidence; it works when participants have
high-quality private information, badly when they do not. Steelman is the procedural
solution for the case where **two well-informed parties disagree and the truth of the
matter is recoverable from the evidence they collectively command**. The procedure
treats the disagreement as a resource, not a problem to be resolved by social pressure.

### Why this doctrine is operationally necessary

The steelman procedure, applied
in advance of an irrevocable decision, is the analogue of public scoring: each
participant must commit to claims that can later be evaluated, and the Adjudicator's
verdict is itself a forecast that can later be checked. This is why the doctrine is
worth the procedural cost.

## 2. The Three Roles

### Champion (Advocate)

**Duty:** make the strongest possible case for the position. Not the most articulate
case, not the most persuasive case — the strongest one, which means the one most
defensible against an informed adversary. The Champion's posture is that of an advocate
in adversarial collaboration: not a debater seeking to win, but a contributor whose job
is to ensure the position they advocate is given full consideration before the joint
process produces a verdict.

**Output discipline per round:**
- Lead with the position. State it in one sentence.
- Articulate the strongest 3-5 supporting arguments. Cite evidence, not consensus.
- Acknowledge the weakest points of the position explicitly. Hiding weaknesses gives the
  Foil free shots and damages credibility for the rest of the procedure. Naming
  weaknesses preemptively is a strength move, not a concession.
- Specify what would change the position: which counter-evidence, which counter-
  argument, which procedural finding by the Adjudicator. This is the Popperian
  falsifiability commitment.

**Posture failures to avoid:**
- Arguing for the position the Champion privately holds while presenting a weaker public
  version. This is sandbagging; it corrupts the procedure.
- Treating the Foil's objections as attacks on the Champion personally. The Foil is
  doing the Champion a service by surfacing what the position must survive.
- Refusing to update across rounds. The Champion's job is to articulate the strongest
  *defensible* form of the position; if the Foil exposes a defect, the Champion's
  Round 3 counter should incorporate the defect's repair, not deny it.

### Foil (Principled Adversary)

**Duty:** produce the strongest principled objections to the Champion's position. "Principled"
is load-bearing: the Foil is not a contrarian; the Foil's objections must themselves be
falsifiable claims with stated evidence and stated conditions under which they would be
withdrawn. A Foil who objects without proposing an alternative or a test is producing
noise.

**Output discipline per round:**
- Open with charitable re-expression (Rapoport's Rule #1). State the Champion's position
  in a form the Champion would endorse. This is the entry condition; a round that
  skips it is procedurally void.
- Identify points of agreement (Rapoport's Rule #2). Most disagreements are local; the
  Foil's job is to narrow the dispute to what actually matters, not to manufacture
  global disagreement.
- Name what the Foil has learned from the Champion's argument (Rapoport's Rule #3).
  This is not flattery; it is a calibration step that surfaces shared updates and
  prevents the next round from rehashing settled ground.
- State the principled objections. Each one carries: the claim, the evidence, the
  conditions under which the Foil would withdraw the objection.

**Posture failures to avoid:**
- Generic objections that apply to any position. "This needs more data" without
  specifying which data is not an objection.
- Contrarianism — objecting to the position because it is the position, not because
  it is wrong. The Foil must be able to articulate, after the verdict, what would
  have made the Foil agree with the Champion. If the answer is "nothing," the Foil
  was a contrarian and the round is void.
- Theatrical aggression. The Foil's tone should be the same as an expert peer
  reviewer's: rigorous, specific, willing to be wrong. Murder-board aggression has its
  place in *preparation* (testing whether the Champion's position survives hostile
  questioning) but is not the right register for a doctrine whose output is supposed
  to be a written record.

### Adjudicator

**Duty:** synthesize the Champion's and Foil's positions into a verdict. The
Adjudicator's verdict is not a vote; it is a written judgment that explains which
arguments survived, which did not, and what the Adjudicator's calibrated confidence is
in the verdict given the evidence the rounds produced. The Adjudicator must be willing
to rule "the question is undecidable from the evidence presented" when that is the
correct answer.

**Output discipline:**
- State the verdict in one sentence.
- For each principled objection the Foil raised: state whether the objection survived,
  was rebutted, or was partially valid. The Adjudicator's job is to make the disposition
  of each objection auditable.
- For each load-bearing argument the Champion advanced: state whether it survived
  Foil scrutiny.
- State the Adjudicator's confidence in the verdict at multiple resolutions: in the
  conclusion, in the supporting reasoning, in the durability of the verdict under
  plausible future evidence. Tetlock's calibration discipline applies — do not round
  to coarse buckets (superforecasters lost accuracy when rounded even to the nearest 5%);
  name the conditions under which the verdict flips.
- Surface methodology debt. The Adjudicator's responsibility extends beyond the
  verdict: where did the procedure produce a weak signal? Which evidence was missing
  that future rounds should target? This is the Heuer move — every analysis closes
  with a list of what would have made the analysis stronger.

**Posture failures to avoid:**
- Splitting the difference. The Adjudicator is not a mediator; producing a synthesis
  that neither party endorses but both can live with is a worse outcome than picking
  one side with confidence and naming the conditions under which the other side wins.
  (The Kahneman-Klein adversarial collaboration concluded with the parties finding they
  **agreed on most of the issues that matter** — "a failure to disagree" — not by
  averaging.)
- Adjudicator capture — the Adjudicator absorbs the senior party's view by default.
  The structural defense is to write the verdict before reading either party's
  response to the verdict draft. If senior status was acquired during the rounds (e.g.,
  the Adjudicator and the Champion are the same person across separate sessions of the
  procedure), the verdict's confidence should be reduced by one tier.
- Refusal to rule. "Both sides have merit; we need more evidence" is sometimes the
  correct verdict, but it is overused as a procedural cop-out. The Adjudicator should
  rule when the evidence supports a ruling and explicitly defer only when the evidence
  is insufficient — and when deferring, must name the specific evidence that would
  resolve the question.

## 3. The Canonical Five Rounds

The five-round procedure is the default. It matches what works empirically: NEJM's peer
review process (initial screen → reviewer round 1 → revision → reviewer round 2 →
editorial decision) produces five distinct adjudicator-relevant moments; NeurIPS and
ICLR (initial review → rebuttal → reviewer discussion → final decision) collapse to
four, but the rebuttal phase carries the load of two rounds (response, then post-
response discussion); registered reports (Stage 1 protocol review → revision → IPA →
Stage 2 results review → revision → final decision) span six moments but compress to
five when the IPA is treated as a procedural commitment rather than a publication.

### Round 1 — Champion's Position

The Champion makes the strongest case for the position. The expected length is
substantial: this is the round where the position is articulated in full, including
weaknesses. The Foil and Adjudicator should have, after Round 1, a written artifact
that an outside reader could use to understand both what is being claimed and why.

Procedural requirements:
- Position statement in one sentence.
- Supporting arguments with evidence.
- Acknowledged weaknesses.
- Falsifiability statement: what would change the position.
- Demands on the Foil: which counter-arguments the Champion expects the Foil to engage
  with, and which the Champion considers settled.

The "demands on the Foil" is borrowed from the legal model of pleading: the Champion
specifies the issues that are genuinely in dispute, so the Foil cannot ambush the
procedure with objections to claims the Champion did not actually make.

### Round 2 — Foil's Principled Objections

The Foil produces the strongest principled objections, opened with the Rapoport-
charitable re-expression of the Champion's position. Length matches Round 1: the Foil's
output is the same artifact-quality as the Champion's, suitable for an outside reader.

Procedural requirements:
- Re-expression of the Champion's position in a form the Champion would endorse.
- Points of agreement.
- Points learned from the Champion's argument.
- Principled objections, each with: claim, evidence, withdrawal condition.
- Demands on the Champion: which objections the Foil expects the Champion to engage with
  in Round 3, and which the Foil acknowledges as marginal.

### Round 3 — Champion's Counter

The Champion responds to the Foil's objections. The Round 3 posture is **responsive**,
not defensive: the Champion must address the Foil's actual objections, not the strawman
the Champion would prefer. If an objection cannot be rebutted, the Champion concedes
it explicitly and revises the position to incorporate the concession.

Procedural requirements:
- For each Foil objection: rebut with new evidence/argument, concede with revised
  position, or defer with explicit reasoning.
- Updated position statement if any concession was made.
- Updated falsifiability statement reflecting the revised position.

Round 3 is where the Champion's intellectual honesty is most tested. A Round 3 that
addresses none of the Foil's actual objections (instead re-asserting Round 1 with more
volume) is procedural collapse; the Adjudicator should rule against the Champion at
verdict.

### Round 4 — Foil's Rebut

The Foil responds to the Champion's counter. The Round 4 posture is **selective**: the
Foil concedes the points the Champion successfully rebutted, sharpens the points that
survive, and identifies any new weaknesses the Champion's Round 3 revisions introduced.

Procedural requirements:
- For each of the Foil's Round 2 objections: concede if Champion's Round 3 rebutted it,
  sharpen if it survives, escalate if the Champion's response made it stronger.
- New objections (if any) to claims the Champion introduced in Round 3.
- A short list of demands on the Adjudicator: which questions the Foil considers
  genuinely undecidable from the evidence, which the Foil considers decisively
  resolved for one side.

Round 4 is where the procedure produces convergence on what remains genuinely
disputed. The output should be a much narrower set of live questions than Round 2
contained.

### Round 5 — Adjudicator's Verdict

The Adjudicator rules. The verdict integrates the four prior rounds and produces:

- The verdict in one sentence.
- Per-objection disposition (the surviving Foil objections from Round 4).
- Per-argument disposition (the load-bearing Champion arguments from Round 3).
- Calibrated confidence in the verdict, supporting reasoning, and durability.
- Methodology debt: weak signals, missing evidence, structural improvements to the
  next iteration of the procedure.

The Round 5 verdict is the procedural output. Downstream consumers cite the verdict;
upstream actors are accountable to the verdict; future iterations of the question are
gated by the conditions the verdict named.

## 4. Stable Convergence: When Five Rounds Is Wrong

### Convergence before five

The procedure can terminate before Round 5 when the rounds **converge**: positions stop
moving, the Foil has no new objections, the Champion has no new arguments, and the
remaining disagreement is acknowledged by both parties as undecidable from current
evidence. Convergence is a positive outcome — it means the procedure has done its job
and the Adjudicator can rule on a smaller, sharper question set.

Signs of convergence:
- Round 3's concessions exceed Round 3's rebuttals (the Champion is updating more than
  rebutting; positions are merging).
- Round 4's "objections withdrawn" exceeds "objections sharpened" (the Foil is
  conceding more than escalating).
- Both parties name the same questions as the live ones (the dispute has narrowed to a
  shared set of unresolved issues).

When all three appear by end of Round 3, the procedure can compress: Round 4 becomes
joint identification of the residual questions, Round 5 is the Adjudicator's ruling on
those questions.

### When five is not enough

The procedure should extend beyond five rounds when:

- **The position keeps moving.** If the Champion's Round 3 introduced material new
  claims, the Foil's Round 4 should be a fresh Round 2 against the new position, and
  the procedure adds rounds (Round 5 = new Champion counter, Round 6 = Foil rebut,
  Round 7 = adjudication).
- **A factual question is resolvable but unresolved.** When the live question depends
  on a piece of evidence the rounds have surfaced but not produced, the right move is to
  pause the procedure, gather the evidence, and resume.
- **The Adjudicator cannot rule with adequate confidence.** A draft verdict at low
  confidence is a signal to extend the procedure, not to ship the low-confidence
  verdict. The Adjudicator should name the specific question that needs more
  argumentation and request a targeted additional round.

The doctrine's commitment to five is a default, not a floor; the doctrine's commitment
to **structured rounds with explicit role discipline** is the floor.

### When the procedure should be abandoned

Three failure modes mean the procedure should be stopped, not continued:

1. **Bad-faith participation.** A Champion who sandbags, a Foil who contrarianizes, or
   an Adjudicator who has predetermined the verdict. The doctrine cannot produce a
   reliable output from bad-faith inputs; stop and reconstitute the participants.
2. **Question malformed.** The procedure has surfaced that the original question is
   ill-formed — the question is actually two questions, or the question depends on
   definitions neither party agrees to. Stop, reformulate the question, restart.
3. **Evidence asymmetry.** One party has access to evidence the other does not, and the
   evidence cannot be shared (classification, IP, personal communications). The
   procedure produces a verdict in the form of "given the evidence available, X" but
   the verdict's reliability is bounded by the evidence asymmetry; the doctrine should
   flag this explicitly and the consumer should weight accordingly.

## 5. Variants by Question Type

The procedure is the same; the round content varies by what is being adjudicated.

### Variant A: Slate / Plan Decisions

The question: "should we do this plan as drafted?" The Champion advocates the plan as
drafted; the Foil identifies the strongest alternatives or modifications; the
Adjudicator rules on the slate. A worked internal case study follows this variant
exactly — the question was an OWASP-slate prioritization decision, the
Champion's Round 1 was the proposed A04 / A05 / A07 / A01 inclusion, the Foil's
Round 2 was the case for A03 swap-in, the Adjudicator's Round 5 ruling was the priority-
floor framing.

Distinguishing feature of this variant: **the live questions are usually inclusion/
exclusion** decisions rather than truth claims. The Adjudicator's verdict is the
revised slate with explicit rationale per inclusion.

### Variant B: PR / Artifact / Code Decisions

The question: "ship this artifact as-is, or send back?" The Champion advocates shipping;
the Foil advocates revision or rejection; the Adjudicator rules on ship-readiness. The
PR steelman battery in the May Workflow Coverage cohort follows this variant.

Distinguishing feature: **the Foil's objections must be concrete and actionable**.
"This needs more testing" without specifying the test that would change the verdict is
out of bounds. "This needs a Go-SQLi corpus positive before the regression-defense
framing is supported" is in bounds — it names a specific evidence demand whose
satisfaction would flip the verdict.

### Variant C: Research Hypothesis Decisions

The question: "is this hypothesis true?" The Champion advocates the hypothesis; the
Foil advocates the null or an alternative; the Adjudicator rules on which the evidence
supports. This is the academic peer-review variant; it maps directly onto the
Kahneman-Klein adversarial collaboration.

Distinguishing feature: **the rounds may demand new evidence collection between them**.
A research-hypothesis steelman that surfaces "we need experiment X to resolve the
dispute" produces a pause, an experiment, and a resumption. Pre-registration (per the
COS Registered Reports model) is the right discipline: both parties commit, before the
experiment, to what outcome would support each position.

### Variant D: Operational / Go-No-Go Decisions

The question: "do we proceed with this action or halt?" The Champion advocates proceed;
the Foil advocates halt; the Adjudicator rules with explicit cost-of-being-wrong
attached to each direction.

Distinguishing feature: **time pressure is a procedural input**. When a decision must
be made in hours, the doctrine compresses to two rounds plus verdict (single round each
of Champion and Foil, then Adjudicator). When time is available, the full five-round
procedure runs. The compression should be explicit; the Adjudicator's confidence in
the compressed-procedure verdict is lower by one tier.

## 6. Procedural Discipline

### Pre-registration of criteria

Before Round 1 opens, the Adjudicator publishes the **standard of review**: which
considerations carry weight, which do not, and at what resolution the Adjudicator will
rule. This is the doctrine's analogue of COS
Registered Reports' pre-committed analysis plan. The discipline is structural defense
against post-hoc rationalization: an Adjudicator who can move the goalposts at Round 5
to favor either party has corrupted the procedure.

A serviceable standard-of-review opens with: "I will weight (1) ... (2) ... (3) ...
[Pre-committed considerations]. I will not weight (a) ... (b) ... [Excluded
considerations]. I will rule at the resolution of [conclusion / per-claim / per-
objection]." The pre-registration is published, then the rounds run.

### Charitable re-expression as the entry condition

Every round after Round 1 opens with a charitable re-expression of the prior round.
Failure to re-express is procedural cause to void the round and require a re-do. The
discipline produces three effects:

1. **It enforces engagement.** A Foil who cannot charitably re-express the Champion's
   position has not understood it well enough to object; the failure to re-express
   surfaces the misunderstanding.
2. **It prevents drift.** Without re-expression, each round can subtly redefine the
   prior round's claims; the discussion ends up not being about the original position.
3. **It improves the procedural record.** A future reader of the rounds gets, in each
   round's opening, a fresh summary of the state of the debate at that point.

The Rapoport-Dennett four-step formulation is the canonical form: re-express, list
agreements, name what was learned, then criticize. The doctrine adopts the four steps
verbatim as the obligatory opening of Rounds 2, 3, and 4.

### Calibrated language

Every claim — Champion's argument, Foil's objection, Adjudicator's verdict — carries an
explicit confidence at one of three resolutions: **high** (>80% credence given the
evidence), **medium** (40-80%), **low** (<40%). Tetlock's tournament evidence is
load-bearing: superforecasters lose accuracy when their forecasts are rounded even to the nearest
5%, but
three-tier confidence is sufficient for the steelman procedure because the rounds are
the calibration mechanism — claims that survive multiple rounds are higher confidence
than claims that don't.

The Adjudicator's verdict additionally states the **conditions under which it flips**:
which evidence, which counter-argument, which procedural change. Without this, the
verdict is not falsifiable in Popper's sense; the doctrine treats it as advisory
rather than dispositive.

### Conflict-of-interest handling

The Champion should not be the Adjudicator. When the same person plays both roles
across separate sessions of the procedure (as is common when a single researcher
steelmans their own conclusion), the verdict's confidence is reduced by one tier and
the methodology debt section must explicitly note the conflict. The structural defense
is to have a second person play the Adjudicator role; absent that, the Adjudicator-as-
self should write the verdict before consulting the rounds, then revise after reading
them.

### Written record discipline

Every round produces a written artifact. Async procedures (where rounds are written
over hours or days) are the default; sync procedures (where rounds happen in a single
meeting) require recording or note-taking that produces the artifact post-hoc.

The artifact format is non-negotiable: the rounds are reproducible in writing or they
did not happen.

Naming and storage discipline:
- `artifacts/steelman_<question>/round{1..N}.md` for the per-round artifacts.
- `artifacts/steelman_<question>/verdict.md` for the Adjudicator's verdict (which may be
  the same file as `round5.md`).
- `data/<question>_steelman_transcript.md` for any sync-meeting transcript that backed
  the artifacts.

## 7. Common Failure Modes

### Strawmanning

The most common failure: a round criticizes a position the prior round did not take.
The structural defense is the Rapoport re-expression requirement; the procedural
defense is the Adjudicator's authority to void a round that strawmans and require it
re-done. Strawmanning is not a stylistic flaw; it is the failure of the procedure to
produce a reliable output.

A specific high-frequency variant: **proxy strawmanning** — criticizing a more
defensible position the prior round did not take, because criticizing that position is
where the Foil's strongest arguments are. The Foil who does this is implicitly
conceding that the Champion's actual position survives the Foil's strongest arguments;
the verdict should reflect that concession.

### Role collapse

When the Champion becomes a Foil ("actually, I'm not sure about this position myself")
or the Foil becomes a Champion ("you've convinced me, here's how to strengthen the
argument"), the procedure stops working as designed. The doctrine's response:
**named role-switching is allowed but must be procedurally explicit**. A Champion who
genuinely updates mid-procedure should declare "I am withdrawing the position and
adopting the Foil's framing"; the procedure then either ends (the question is resolved
in the Foil's favor) or restarts with a new Champion (if the prior Foil's position is
itself contestable).

Unmarked role-collapse is the more common failure: the parties converge without
declaring it, and the resulting verdict is unmoored from any clearly-defended position.
The Adjudicator should flag this and either reconstitute the parties or void the
procedure.

### Theater

Going through the motions of a steelman without engagement. The signal is that the
rounds repeat each other with stylistic variation; no actual updating happens; the
verdict was effectively determined before Round 1. Theater is more common when the
steelman is performed for an audience (a written record to be shown to stakeholders)
than when it is performed for decision support.

The structural defense is to require, in each round, **at least one named update from
the prior round** — a sentence of the form "in light of the prior round's argument, I
now believe X where I previously believed Y." A round with zero updates is suspicious;
two consecutive rounds with zero updates are theatrical.

### Adjudicator capture

The Adjudicator absorbs one party's view by default. Most common when the Adjudicator
is the senior person and the Champion is junior (the Champion's framing becomes the
verdict by deference) or when the Adjudicator and one party share an institutional
incentive (the verdict aligns with the incentive). The procedural defense is the
pre-registered standard of review and the requirement that the verdict name the
conditions under which it flips; a verdict whose flip-conditions are vague is a
captured verdict.

### Premature convergence

The procedure converges before the live questions have been adequately argued.
Distinguishable from genuine convergence by the asymmetry: in premature convergence,
one party concedes points the other party did not push hard on; in genuine convergence,
the concessions follow demonstrated arguments. The Adjudicator's job is to refuse
convergence that lacks supporting argument, even when both parties seem willing to
converge.

### Foil-as-contrarian

The Foil objects without proposing an alternative or a test. The objection is not
falsifiable (no condition would withdraw it) and not actionable (no remediation would
satisfy it). The Adjudicator should treat such objections as procedurally void and rule
without them. The Foil who consistently produces contrarian objections should be
replaced.

### Adjudicator paralysis

The Adjudicator refuses to rule because the evidence is incomplete. This is appropriate
when the evidence is genuinely insufficient; it is overused as a way to avoid
controversy. The discipline is: when the Adjudicator defers, the verdict must specify
the evidence that would resolve the question and the procedure for gathering it. A
"both sides have merit, more research is needed" verdict without specification is
indistinguishable from procedural failure.

## 8. Operating Model

### When to steelman vs. just decide

The procedural cost of a five-round steelman is non-trivial — typically half a day of
attention from each participant for a high-stakes decision, an hour or two for a
compressed variant. The doctrine is worth its cost when:

- The decision is **hard to reverse** (architectural choices, slate commitments,
  policy positions, hiring, public statements).
- **Stakeholders disagree** and the disagreement is on the merits rather than on
  preference.
- The decision will be **questioned later** and the procedural record is itself an
  output (audit defense, post-mortem input).

The doctrine is wasted when:

- The decision is **routine** and well-understood (the steelman is theater).
- The decision is **time-bounded by external factors** (the doctrine's compressed
  variant is appropriate, not the full procedure).
- One side is **clearly correct** and the disagreement is performative.

The auditor's heuristic: would the participants in this disagreement be willing to put
money against their position? If yes, the doctrine is appropriate. If no, the
disagreement is preference, not evidence — settle it by vote, by senior authority, or
by coin flip, but not by steelman.

### Solo steelman

A solo steelman is the doctrine performed by one person across all three roles, in
sequence. It is the most common variant in practice because it is cheap; it is also the
most failure-prone because role-discipline depends on the same person genuinely
inhabiting three positions. The structural defenses are:

- **Separation in time.** Write Round 1 in the morning; write Round 2 (Foil) after a
  break of several hours or the next day. Cold context aids role separation.
- **Voicing.** Each role is written in a distinctive register — Champion in the first
  person, Foil in the second person addressing the Champion, Adjudicator in the third
  person. The voicing discipline cues the role-switching mentally.
- **External Adjudicator.** Even when the rounds are solo, the verdict is read by a
  second person who is asked specifically to identify which arguments were strawmanned
  or under-engaged. A solo procedure with external Adjudicator review is comparable in
  reliability to a multi-person procedure with internal Adjudicator.

### Multi-person steelman

When the stakes warrant, the three roles are played by three different people. The
Foil should not be the most junior person on the team (whose objections will be
discounted); the Champion should not be the originator of the position (who is
psychologically committed); the Adjudicator should not be the most senior person on
the team (whose verdict will not be challenged). The right defaults:

- **Champion**: a person with strong knowledge of the position but no personal stake in
  it.
- **Foil**: a person with strong knowledge of the domain and a documented track record
  of identifying defects. Often the team's most-respected critic.
- **Adjudicator**: a person whose authority derives from procedural discipline, not
  from seniority. May rotate across rounds of unrelated steelmans to prevent
  Adjudicator-bias accumulation.

### Async vs. sync

Async (rounds spread over days) is the default for written procedures; sync (rounds in
a single meeting) is the default for time-critical procedures. The Adjudicator's
discretion governs.

Async strengths: more time per round, deeper evidence-gathering between rounds,
written-first artifact production.
Async weaknesses: drift between rounds, participant attrition, slower convergence.

Sync strengths: tight feedback loop, less drift, faster convergence.
Sync weaknesses: social dynamics dominate, the loudest voice wins absent strict
moderation, the written record is reconstructed post-hoc and loses fidelity.

The mixed mode — sync rounds with written artifacts produced before each meeting —
captures most of the benefits of both. The standing recommendation is sync rounds
preceded by async written drafts; the meeting is for the actual exchange, the writeup
is the record.

### Cross-link with adjacent doctrines

The steelman doctrine composes with several adjacent practices in this scaffolding:

- **[[reference-code-review-expertise]]** — code review's candidate-point methodology
  is itself a steelman: the auditor (Foil) tests the developer's (Champion) implicit
  claim that the code is secure, and the verdict is the audit finding. The doctrine's
  per-round discipline can be applied to a complex finding-with-pushback exchange.
- **[[feedback-research-discipline]]** — steelman is the procedure that research
  discipline calls for when a research finding is contested; the artifact-first
  workflow naturally produces steelman-quality records.
- **[[llm-agent-security-expertise]]** — the agent-security doctrine adopts the
  Foil role explicitly as "adaptive attacker"; threat-modeling agentic systems
  uses the OWASP Agentic Top 10 categories (see Section 9) as the Foil's
  cross-check list.
- **[[agentic-web-capabilities-expertise]]** — capability-bound agent design
  borrows directly from CaMeL (see Section 9.2); the dual-LLM privileged-vs-
  quarantined split is the architectural instantiation of role separation.
- **[[adversarial-ml-model-security-expertise]]** — adaptive-attack methodology
  (Section 9.3) is the steelman doctrine applied to model robustness claims;
  "attacker moves second" is the canonical Foil discipline.
- **[[game-theory-mechanism-design-security-expertise]]** — the multi-round
  Champion / Foil structure is the security analogue of a Stackelberg
  commitment-then-best-response game; defender-moves-first and
  attacker-moves-second is the canonical adversarial game in modern AI safety
  evaluation.
- **Adversarial collaboration** (Kahneman-Klein) — the multi-party variant of the
  doctrine extended over a research program rather than a single decision. When
  positions remain unresolved across multiple steelmans, the parties may escalate to
  adversarial collaboration: agreeing in advance on the experiments that would resolve
  the question and conducting them jointly. The 2025 Isch-Tetlock-Clark survey of
  29 scholars across 13 completed adversarial-collaboration projects (see
  Section 9.7) is the most recent empirical validation that the method is worth
  its procedural cost: 27 of 29 participants said they would do it again, and
  the modal outcome was "a deeper understanding of the problem space through
  integration of opposing perspectives" rather than a victor declared.

### Closing — what staff-level looks like

A staff-level practitioner of this doctrine is not measured by the verdicts they win,
or by the elegance of their Champion arguments, or by the sharpness of their Foil
objections. They are measured by **how often their decisions, made with the procedure,
hold up under future evidence**. The procedure is worth its cost only insofar as it
produces decisions that were better than the decisions the same people would have made
without it.

Tetlock's calibration discipline is the right benchmark: a practitioner whose Round 5
verdicts at medium confidence are right 60% of the time, and whose high-confidence
verdicts are right 85% of the time, is calibrated. A practitioner whose verdicts are
right 95% of the time is sandbagging confidence; whose verdicts are right 30% of the
time is producing noise. The doctrine's goal is calibration, not infallibility — and
calibration is achievable only when the verdicts can be checked against future
evidence, which is why **the verdict's flip-conditions are themselves the most
important output of the procedure**.

## 9. Steelman doctrine applied to AI safety and AI red-teaming (2024-2026)

The steelman doctrine pre-dates the AI safety field by decades, but the period
2024-2026 produced the first generation of tools, frameworks, and empirical
results that operationalize the three-role discipline at the scale and tempo
that frontier AI development requires. This section catalogs the contemporary
instantiations and what each contributes to the doctrine.

### 9.1 Anthropic Petri — the auditor / target / judge separation as a productized doctrine

Petri (Parallel Exploration Tool for Risky Interactions) is an open-source
auditing tool released by Anthropic on 2025-10-06 and donated to Meridian
Labs in 2026 to ensure independence from any AI lab
(alignment.anthropic.com/2025/petri/; anthropic.com/research/donating-
open-source-petri). Petri is the clearest contemporary instantiation of the
three-role doctrine in software form: an **auditor** model probes a **target**
model through multi-turn conversations with synthetic tools, conversation
rollback, and prefill steering; a separate **judge** model scores the
resulting transcripts across 36 default dimensions (deception, sycophancy,
power-seeking, and others) on 1-10 scales. The pilot used 111 seed
instructions across 14 frontier models with five models rotating through
auditor and judge roles in 25 combinations per instruction, yielding 2,775
scores per target.

The role mapping to this doctrine is direct: the **auditor is the Foil**, the
**target is the Champion** (defending the implicit claim "I behave aligned"),
and the **judge is the Adjudicator**. The rotation of models across the
auditor and judge roles is the structural defense against Adjudicator capture
(Section 6) — no single model can preferentially favor a target it shares
training data with. The donation to Meridian Labs is the structural defense
against institutional Adjudicator capture: an evaluation tool maintained by
the lab whose models it evaluates carries lower epistemic weight than one
maintained by an independent nonprofit.

Production deployments have a third Adjudicator-capture failure mode that
Petri's eval-harness rotation does not address: **single-team ownership of
tool + threat model + controls**. When one team builds the AI tool, authors
the threat model, and operates the runtime controls, the Adjudicator is
structurally captured before any model bias enters — the group scoring
whether the tool is "safe enough" is the same group whose deliverable is the
tool itself. Model rotation (different model per role) and the Meridian Labs
donation (different organization owning the harness) are structural
rotations along orthogonal axes; the production-side analogue is rotation of
*reviewing authority*, not of model identity. The four-actor threat split in
`[[reference-ai-assisted-coding-tool-security]]` §2 inherits this hazard:
the defender operationalizing each row (developer-as-attacker, repo-as-
attacker, upstream-LLM-as-attacker, middleware-MCP-as-attacker) plays
Adjudicator without structural rotation, and the failure mode is the same
shape as Anthropic-evaluating-Anthropic before the donation. The
operational mitigations translate directly from Petri's doctrine:
adversarial external review for AI tools that an internal team controls;
separating the AppSec team that owns the tool from the GRC function or
external auditor that owns the threat-model sign-off; staffing incident-
review boards at least partially from outside the owning team. Each is a
production-side rotation that the steelman discipline classifies the same
way: an unrotated Adjudicator is a captured Adjudicator, whether the
captor is a model, an institution, or a single accountable team.

Petri 2.0 (alignment.anthropic.com/2026/petri-v2/) added improved
eval-awareness mitigations — the target model attempting to detect that it is
being audited and behave differently — which is the AI-safety analogue of
**theater** (Section 7). The mitigations: a realism classifier that checks auditor behavior each
turn and requests a rewrite of anything implausible, plus manual seed
revisions, so the interactions look and feel more like real usage. The doctrine's defense
against theater (require at least one named update per round) translates
directly: a target that produces identical responses to identical inputs
across audited and unaudited contexts is suspected of eval-awareness theater.

### 9.2 CaMeL — architectural instantiation of role separation

"Defeating Prompt Injections by Design" by Debenedetti, Shumailov, Fan,
Hayes, Carlini, Fabian, Kern, Shi, Terzis, and Tramèr (Google DeepMind /
Google Research / ETH Zürich), arXiv:2503.18813 (2025), proposes **CaMeL**
(CApabilities for MachinE Learning) — a defense layer that splits the LLM
runtime into a **privileged** component that processes trusted user queries
and manages control flow, and a **quarantined** component that processes
untrusted retrieved data and is blocked from influencing program execution.
A custom Python interpreter tracks data and instruction provenance through
the system; capability-based security policies enforce that data from
quarantined sources cannot be passed to sensitive tools. The paper reports
77% task completion with provable security on AgentDojo versus 84% for
undefended baselines — a small capability tax for a structural defense.

CaMeL's architectural shape is the doctrine's role-separation principle
realized in code: the privileged LLM is the **Champion** (carrying the
user's intent forward); the quarantined LLM is the **Foil** in the
limited sense that its outputs are treated as adversarial and must be
sanitized before reuse; the capability system is the **Adjudicator**
(deciding which data flows are permitted given the user's pre-registered
policy). The pre-registration of capability policy before the agent runs
is the same structural move as the doctrine's pre-registered standard
of review (Section 6) — the rules that decide which calls survive must be
fixed before the rounds begin, not chosen post-hoc.

### 9.3 "The Attacker Moves Second" — adaptive-attack discipline as institutionalized steelman

Nasr, Carlini, Sitawarin et al., arXiv:2510.09023 (2025-10), tested 12
recent LLM-defense techniques against adaptive attackers that explicitly
modify their strategy to counter each defense's design. **Defenses claiming
near-zero attack success rates in their original papers collapsed to >90%
attack success under adaptive attack** — gradient descent, reinforcement
learning, random search, and human-guided exploration applied with
defense-specific knowledge. The paper's title is its thesis: defenses must
be evaluated by attackers who move second, after the defense is published,
not by attackers who move first against an unspecified target.

This is the doctrine's falsifiability commitment (Section 1, irreducible
commitment #2) applied to AI security claims. A defense whose authors
cannot name the adaptive attack that would defeat it is producing a
non-falsifiable claim; the steelman procedure treats such claims as
advisory rather than dispositive. The paper's empirical headline — a
typical 90+ percentage-point gap between static and adaptive evaluation —
is the load-bearing justification for the doctrine in the AI safety
context: **without the Foil moving second, the Champion's claims are
unverified by construction**.

The paper has become methodologically canonical: subsequent defense papers
in 2025-2026 are increasingly expected to include an explicit "adaptive
adversary" section in their evaluation, paralleling the way Popperian
falsifiability became expected in physical-science manuscripts after the
1960s. This is the steelman doctrine institutionalizing itself in a new
field.

### 9.4 NIST AI 600-1 — the four-function framework as scaffolding for the doctrine

NIST AI 600-1, *Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative
Artificial Intelligence Profile* (NIST, 2024-07-26,
nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf), identifies twelve risk
areas for generative AI (CBRN information, confabulation, dangerous content,
data privacy, environmental impacts, harmful bias and homogenization,
human-AI configuration, information integrity, information security,
intellectual property, obscene or degrading content, and value chain and
component integration) with 200+ suggested actions mapped to the four AI
RMF core functions: **Govern, Map, Measure, Manage**.

The four functions decompose the doctrine: **Govern** is the pre-registration
of standard of review (Section 6); **Map** is the Champion's Round 1
position-statement and falsifiability commitment; **Measure** is the Foil's
Round 2 evidence-anchored objection set; **Manage** is the Adjudicator's
Round 5 verdict with explicit flip-conditions. NIST AI 600-1 is the first
US-federal-policy document that codifies the doctrine's structural
discipline as governance scaffolding. It does not invent the doctrine; it
makes it auditable at organizational scale.

### 9.5 OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications 2026 — categorized Foil checklist

OWASP Gen AI Security Project, *Top 10 for Agentic Applications* (published
2025-12-09, genai.owasp.org), identifies ten primary risk categories for
agentic AI systems: **ASI01 Agent Goal Hijack**, **ASI02 Tool Misuse and
Exploitation**, **ASI03 Identity and Privilege Abuse**, **ASI04 Agentic
Supply Chain Vulnerabilities**, **ASI05 Unexpected Code Execution**, **ASI06
Memory and Context Poisoning**, **ASI07 Insecure Inter-Agent Communication**,
**ASI08 Cascading Failures**, **ASI09 Human-Agent Trust Exploitation**, and
**ASI10 Rogue Agents**. The document was developed by more than 100
industry experts, researchers, and practitioners.

For the doctrine's purposes, the OWASP Top 10 is the **Foil's
cross-reference checklist** for any agentic-system steelman: the Foil's
Round 2 objections, to be considered procedurally complete, must
explicitly engage with each of the ten categories that applies to the
target. A Foil who objects to an agent's tool-use safety without
addressing ASI02 (tool misuse) and ASI04 (supply-chain compromise) has
produced a procedurally incomplete objection. The Top 10's **"least agency"**
principle — grant agents the minimum autonomy required for safe, bounded
tasks — is the agentic-AI version of the doctrine's stance that confidence
must be earned via the procedure, not assumed by default.

### 9.6 DARPA AIxCC — the doctrine at scale, with real money

The DARPA AI Cyber Challenge final competition culminated at DEF CON 33
(August 2025) with seven finalist teams running cyber reasoning systems
against 54 million lines of code (darpa.mil/news/2025/aixcc-results). The
scoring algorithm weighted **patching** at 3× the value of **identifying**
a vulnerability — a structural commitment that a Champion-claim ("I have
found a defect") must be paired with a Foil-grade demonstration that
the defect can be repaired without breaking functionality, not merely
exposed. Systems identified 54 of 63 synthetic vulnerabilities (86%) and
patched 43 (68%); they also surfaced 18 previously unknown real-world
flaws across the corpus. Team Atlanta's ATLANTIS won with $4M; Trail of
Bits and Theori took second and third.

AIxCC is the doctrine running as a **tournament**, which Tetlock's
*Superforecasting* (2015) work argues is the most reliable mechanism for
narrowing confident-but-uncalibrated claims to calibrated, evidence-anchored
ones. The patch-weighting structure is the procedural enforcement that
Champion and Foil contributions are not equally valued — the doctrine
accepts asymmetry when it is principled. The competition produced empirical
support for a long-running steelman question in cyber-AI: **does an
adversarial-AI system reduce or increase systemic security?** The 18
real-world findings plus 11 real-world patches across the seven systems
support a tentative "reduce" verdict, subject to flip-conditions about
operational deployment cost (~$152 per task average) and false-positive
patch rate.

### 9.7 Adversarial-collaboration update — Isch, Tetlock, & Clark (2025)

Calvin Isch, Philip Tetlock, and Cory Clark, "Reflections on adversarial
collaboration from the adversaries: was it worth it?" (*Theory and Society*,
2025; PMC12748294), surveyed and interviewed 29 scholars across 13 completed
adversarial-collaboration projects identified through systematic literature
search. Key findings:

- **27 of 29 scholars said they would do it again.** This is the strongest
  recent empirical signal that the doctrine's procedural cost is worth
  paying.
- **Conflicts were "surprisingly rare" and mostly minor.** The doctrine's
  charitable-re-expression discipline (Section 6) is doing the work; absent
  it, interpersonal conflict scales with intellectual disagreement.
- **The modal outcome was not victor-declaration but problem-space
  integration.** This is the convergence model from Section 4 — a successful
  procedure narrows the dispute, it does not always settle it.
- **About 34% reported belief updating; about 41% called it
  complicated.** Calibration moves in the direction the doctrine
  predicts: high-confidence positions become medium-confidence with named
  flip-conditions, not "everyone changed their mind."
- **Peer-review time was reduced.** A pre-coordinated adversarial pair
  produces a manuscript whose objections have been engaged before
  submission, which collapses the article-comment-reply cycle.

The 2025 reflections paper also notes a selection-bias caveat the doctrine
should respect: the study covers only **successful** adversarial
collaborations; participants in abandoned projects did not respond. Survival
bias inflates the satisfaction estimate; the doctrine's posture should be
"the method works when it completes, and completion is not guaranteed."

Tetlock's parallel work on **AI-risk adversarial collaborations** (2024-2025,
Forecasting Research Institute) ran the same method between AI domain
experts and superforecasters on existential-risk timelines. Neither group
moved its long-term estimates, but both pre-registered **cruxes** —
specific 2030-resolvable indicators that would shift each side's view.
This is the doctrine's Round 5 flip-condition discipline (Section 6)
applied across years and across communities; the 2030 resolution events
are themselves the procedural output.

### 9.8 What the AI-safety doctrine inherits, and what it adds

The 2024-2026 instantiations preserve the doctrine's irreducible
commitments (charity, falsifiability, provisional adjudication) and add
four operational adaptations:

1. **Roles can be machines.** Petri's auditor / judge separation
   demonstrates that two of the three doctrine roles can be played by
   automated systems at scale, provided the judge is rotated across
   models to prevent capture. The Champion role — the target system being
   evaluated — remains structurally fixed.
2. **Architectural enforcement complements procedural enforcement.** CaMeL
   demonstrates that role separation can be built into the system's
   runtime, not only into the human procedure. A privileged-vs-quarantined
   split enforces capability-based security regardless of whether the
   reviewers follow the doctrine.
3. **The Foil must be allowed to move second.** The attacker-moves-second
   paper makes explicit what the doctrine has always assumed: a Foil that
   evaluates a position only against pre-published attacks produces a
   weaker verdict than a Foil that can craft objections after seeing the
   defense. This is the same insight as the doctrine's rule that Round 2
   must engage with Round 1's actual claims, not pre-decided
   counter-positions.
4. **Tournaments scale calibration.** AIxCC and the Good Judgment Project
   (Tetlock, 2011-2015) both demonstrate that multi-party tournaments with
   accountability produce better-calibrated outputs than single-pair
   debates. The doctrine accommodates this: when N steelmans on related
   questions can be aggregated, the resulting verdicts are more reliable
   than any single five-round procedure.

What the AI safety field has **not yet produced** is a robust analogue of
the pre-registered-trial discipline from COS Registered Reports. The 2025
reflections paper and the AI-risk adversarial collaborations both note
that pre-registration of resolution criteria remains uneven; defense
papers generally do not pre-register the adaptive attacks they would
consider falsifying. This is the next frontier for the doctrine in the AI
safety context: institutional adoption of pre-registered Foil criteria
parallel to NEJM's pre-registered review criteria for clinical trials.
